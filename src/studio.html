About GENChase

GENChase is a generative art studio built from real scientific simulations. Every tab is a pattern-forming system that physicists, chemists, biologists and mathematicians actually study, implemented from its governing equations rather than imitated, and opened up so you can compose with it.

What makes it different

Each tab names its scientific sources; implementation accuracy and validation coverage vary. Three-vortex collapse bound, the parallelogram lock, and the quincunx lock are worked derivations of classical dynamics, with historical priority unconfirmed, and locked so the status line marks miss if the claim is wrong. Miss is a grade on the numbers, not a crash: the picture still draws. What is unusual about the rest is the assembly. 130 sciences share one control surface, one seed field, one palette editor and one export pipeline, so moving between chemistry and acoustics costs you nothing. Each technique exposes dozens of parameters spanning subtle to broken, not the four sliders a demo needs to illustrate one behavior. And everything is deterministic: a seed plus its parameters reproduces a piece exactly, at any size, forever, with seeded random draws. Vector geometry scales; numerical grids retain finite resolution and may differ across precision or hardware.

Made to be printed

The print control reads in inches and centimeters as well as pixels, because the output is meant for paper. Fine-art giclée printers accept 300 to 360 ppi natively, which is the real ceiling of visible quality, so those are the defaults. Turn on Colophon in the print bar (or in the export sheet) and the file becomes a mounted plate: the image inset in a paper margin, and beneath it the technique, its governing equation, the seed, the palette and every parameter. The sheet offers PNG (the archival file), JPEG and WebP of the same pixels, and SVG where the plate is vectors. A living plate, or a still one that can run, also offers an 8-second clip of the canvas (WebM, or MP4 where that is all the browser encodes). That is the plate in time, not a print. Scientific plates carried their own method for centuries and generative art almost never does. The piece on the wall states how it was made and could be remade.

How to use it

Press Generate or the space bar for a new seed. Surprise me randomizes the whole parameter space within ranges that usually land somewhere good. Presets are starting points, not endpoints. The URL hash is the recipe: technique, seed and parameters, so you can send someone a plate. Copy link puts that URL on the clipboard. Settings JSON is the verbose fallback (schema v: 1). Session history under the tabs holds the last plates; Timeline hides that strip (H). Clear on the left of it empties it. Reset restores the current technique to its factory plate. Focus (F) hides the chrome so the plate fills the window; Esc brings it back. Click the seed label to copy it. P pauses a running plate. Record (V) films a clip of up to 60 seconds as WebM, or MP4 where the browser only encodes that. It appears on a living plate, and on a still plate that can run — pressing it starts the field. The export sheet also offers an 8-second clip, plus JPEG and WebP of the print. Ambient (A) enters focus and walks the techniques every 30 seconds with a fresh seed, for a second monitor or a wall; Esc stops it. Click or drag to disturb the field. The LIVE badge means the simulation is still computing; Still means this frame is finished. Click the badge, or press W, to cycle it down to a bare dot and then out of the way; it stays where you left it. The arrows at the lower right move around the plate, still or live; + and − zoom, Fit or 0 puts it back. Alt-drag or the middle button pans; the wheel zooms toward the pointer. This is a look, not a recipe: the print is always the full plate. Save (B) keeps a plate in the gallery; Gallery (G) opens it. , and . walk the preset list without opening the menu. C copies the plate to the clipboard at screen size, for pasting into a message; E is still the route to a file at print resolution. The techniques

  • Artificial Life — Lenia and Flow Lenia, continuous cellular automata whose creatures self-organize; the flow variant conserves mass exactly, so they compete for a fixed budget of substance. Chan, 2019–23.
  • Physarum 3D — slime mold agents building a filament network through a volume, raymarched. The same algorithm astronomers used in 2020 to map the cosmic web.
  • Physarum — the two-dimensional transport network. Jones, 2010.
  • Differential Growth — curves that buckle because they grow faster than the space holding them, the way coral and cortex fold.
  • Fractal Geometry — signed-distance raymarching of the Mandelbulb, Mandelbox, Menger sponge and Apollonian gasket.
  • Neural Patterns — compositional pattern-producing networks evaluated per pixel. Stanley, 2007.
  • Chimera States — identical oscillators that split into a synchronized domain beside a chaotic one, thought impossible until 2002.
  • Swarmalators — oscillators that swarm in space and sync in phase at once. Five states from a rainbow ring to an active phase wave. O’Keeffe, Hong and Strogatz, 2017.
  • Chirikov map — the standard map as a density plate: KAM islands in a chaotic sea. Chirikov, 1969.
  • Cahn–Hilliard — spinodal decomposition: a mixture inside the spinodal unmixes itself, then coarsens. Cahn and Hilliard, 1958.
  • Ohta–Kawasaki — Cahn–Hilliard plus a long-range Coulomb term: diblock spots and lamellae of finite size. Ohta and Kawasaki, 1986.
  • Swift–Hohenberg — the universal envelope of Rayleigh–Bénard convection near onset: rolls, hexagons, localized states. Swift and Hohenberg, 1977.
  • Lifshitz–Petrich — two length scales in one free energy; 12-fold quasicrystal from Faraday waves. Lifshitz and Petrich, 1997.
  • Phase-field crystal — the same free energy as Swift–Hohenberg, but conserved: atoms as density peaks, grains, vacancies, freezing. Elder, Katakowski, Haataja and Grant, 2002.
  • Kuramoto–Sivashinsky — the simplest PDE that produces extensive chaos; cellular flame fronts. Kuramoto and Tsuzuki 1976, Sivashinsky 1977.
  • Complex Ginzburg–Landau — the envelope of an oscillatory instability: spirals, defect chaos, frozen vortex glass. Newell, Whitehead, Segel; Aranson and Kramer’s atlas.
  • XY / Kosterlitz–Thouless — planar spins on a lattice whose vortices bind or unbind with temperature. Kosterlitz and Thouless, 1973.
  • Active Nematics — self-driven rods whose +½ and −½ defects dance and unbind into active turbulence. Microtubule–kinesin films; Beris–Edwards plus active stress.
  • Fluid — a stable-fluids Navier-Stokes solver, with the option to show the finite-time Lyapunov ridges that organize the mixing.
  • Dendritic Growth — Kobayashi's phase-field model of solidification: snowflakes and metal dendrites from two coupled fields.
  • Gravner–Griffeath snowflake — mesoscopic lattice snow crystal: vapor, quasi-liquid, ice. Plates, dendrites, ferns. Gravner and Griffeath, 2008.
  • Flow Field — collision-avoiding strokes grown along a noise field, with widths, spacing and palettes under full control.
  • Reaction-Diffusion — the Gray-Scott activator and inhibitor, the chemistry behind Turing's 1952 answer to how a uniform embryo breaks symmetry.
  • Aperiodic Tilings — Penrose rhombs, kites and darts, Ammann–Beenker, and the 2023 hat and spectre monotiles: a 13-gon (with its mirror) and a chiral 14-gon that each tile the plane without ever repeating.
  • Attractors — Lorenz, Rössler, Clifford and de Jong rendered as density maps of millions of iterations.
  • Hofstadter butterfly — Bloch electron in a magnetic field: the almost-Mathieu spectrum, fractal in flux. Hofstadter, 1976.
  • Helmholtz scars — high eigenmodes of a stadium or cardioid, some sitting on unstable periodic orbits. Heller, 1984.
  • Optical caustics — folds and cusps of a light field through a smooth height. Berry.
  • Smectic focal conics — Dupin cyclides packed as confocal ellipse-hyperbola pairs. Friedel, 1910.
  • Hastings–Levitov — Laplacian growth by composing conformal slit maps. Hastings and Levitov, 1998.
  • Lichtenberg — dielectric breakdown, the η-model. Niemeyer, Pietronero, Wiesmann, 1984.
  • Phyllotaxis — primordia on a growing disc, each sited at the inhibitory minimum. Fibonacci from physics. Douady and Couder, 1996.
  • Indra’s Pearls — Schottky limit sets by iterated inversion. Mumford, Series, Wright, 2002.
  • Talbot carpet — near-field self-imaging of a periodic grating. Talbot, 1836.
  • Chladni & Waves — standing waves on a plate, their nodal lines, and the sand that settles along them. Chladni, 1787.
  • Cortical Planforms — a Wilson–Cowan neural field patterning under lateral inhibition, pushed through the retinocortical map: Klüver's form constants out of a Turing bifurcation. Ermentrout and Cowan, 1979.
  • Random Matrices — β-ensemble spectra from the Dumitriu–Edelman tridiagonal models, and Dyson Brownian motion of GOE eigenvalues. Level repulsion as a continuous slider. Dyson, 1962.
  • Drainage Networks — stream-power incision against uplift and soil creep, solved on the donor stack: dendritic river networks and the watersheds they divide. Howard, 1994; Braun and Willett, 2013.
  • Rough Growth — ballistic deposition, RSOS, surface relaxation and Eden clusters: three roughening universality classes, with the exponent fitted from the deposit on the plate. Kardar, Parisi and Zhang, 1986.
  • Foam & Grains — the cellular Potts model coarsening into a froth, with the von Neumann–Mullins n−6 law measured on the plate. Graner and Glazier, 1992.
  • Vortex Lattice — a rotating condensate answering with quantized vortices in an Abrikosov lattice, found from the phase winding rather than from the density. Gross and Pitaevskii, 1961.
  • Flocking — Toner–Tu hydrodynamics of a polar active fluid: clustering, active turbulence, and number fluctuations measured against the equilibrium square root. Toner and Tu, 1995.
  • Hyperbolic Turing — Gray–Scott chemistry on a {p,q} tiling of the hyperbolic plane, drawn in the Poincaré disk, where the area at distance r grows like e^r and a pattern cannot simply repeat.
  • Spanning Trees — a uniform spanning tree of the grid, sampled exactly by Wilson's algorithm, and the loop-erased random walk inside it whose scaling limit is SLE at κ = 2. Wilson, 1996.
  • Force Chains — a soft-sphere granular packing settled under gravity, drawn as the network of contacts that carries the load. Cundall and Strack, 1979; Majmudar and Behringer, 2005.
  • Liesegang Rings — periodic precipitation in a gel: a reaction front that does not deposit smoothly but in bands, with a spacing that grows geometrically. Liesegang, 1896.

The live catalog is 130 techniques. The list above is a sample. The rest is in TECHNIQUES.md.

How it is built

The folder studio uses one shared engine and loads technique files as you open their tabs. Start a launcher in run/ to serve the folder on your own computer. No package installation, framework, external CDN, analytics or tracking is required. The optional dist/studio.html bundles the same studio into one portable file. Simulations use JavaScript and WebGL2. Preserve the studio version with a recipe: changes to solvers, numerical precision, hardware and output resolution can change a plate.

Built by Chaos with Claude. Each technique credits the researchers who worked it out; the validation coverage and remaining limitations are recorded in VALIDATION.md.
