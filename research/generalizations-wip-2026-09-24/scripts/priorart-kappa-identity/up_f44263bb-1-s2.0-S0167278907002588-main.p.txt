Abstract
Conﬁgurations of point vortices in a 2D ﬂuid that are placed at the vertices of concentric regular m-gons, i.e. point vortex rings, are considered.
The number of conﬁgurations that rotate uniformly – relative equilibria – is shown to be ﬁnite in the case of three rings with arbitrary circulations,
subject to a few circulation constraints. Similar ﬁniteness results for collapse conﬁgurations of three rings are also obtained, and an effective
computational method is described.

1. Introduction
Point vortex rings, which are arrangements of point vortices
at the vertices of concentric regular polygons, have been studied
since the nineteenth century; many references can be found
in [1–3]. An arrangement is a relative equilibrium if all vortices
rotate uniformly about the central point, so that the intervortex
distances are constant. These arrangements are analogous to
central conﬁgurations of point masses in celestial mechanics.
Relative equilibria of rings of identical point vortices
have been observed in plasmas and rotating superﬂuids [2],
and have been the object of additional investigation. An
extensive catalog of relative equilibria obtained numerically [4]
contains many ring relative equilibria. More recent work
has focused on analytic descriptions. For example, all such
arrangements containing fewer than 8 identical vortices have
been characterized as the zeros of certain polynomials [5];
many of these are ring conﬁgurations. All relative equilibria of
two and three identical rings, with and without an additional
central vortex, have been determined analytically [3]. The
number of relative equilibria was found to be a function of the
number m of vortices per ring, stabilizing for large m.

Less is known about heterogeneous point vortex ring
conﬁgurations, where the circulations of the vortices differ from
ring to ring. A lower bound on the number of relative equilibria
can be established when all circulations are positive, by a
simple extension of the topological arguments in [11]. Having
circulations of differing sign opens up the possibility of vortex
collapse [2], and indeed collapse conﬁgurations of two rings
are known to exist [7]. For two rings of two vortices each and a
central vortex, one choice of circulations produces a continuous
family of relative equilibria [12].

2. Preliminaries
In this paper an array of identical point vortices located
at the vertices of a regular m-gon with center at the origin
of the complex plane will be called a point vortex ring. The
ring is completely described by three parameters: the integer
m ≥2, the nonzero real circulation Γ and the nonzero complex
coordinate z of one of the vortices. The other vortices in the

zm = (z′)m; this gives an equivalence relation on the punctured
plane and we may use zm as a coordinate.

5. Computation of homographic conﬁgurations
In this section some matters concerning the computation of
relative equilibrium and collapse conﬁgurations are addressed.
Ideally, a numerical scheme for ﬁnding all homographic
conﬁgurations would be computationally efﬁcient, even for
large m, and also comprehensive so as to generate the complete
set of solutions. Some tradeoff between these attributes is
necessary in practice, as will be seen below.
5.1. An upper bound
The ﬁrst question that arises is: how many solutions do we
expect? Given that the number of homographic conﬁgurations
of three rings is ﬁnite, an upper bound for the number of
conﬁgurations can be obtained through the use of the Bezout
theorem for products of projective spaces [13]. Consider the
case ˜K̸ = 0; the other case is similar. Eqs. (L3a) and (L3b)
each consist of two coefﬁcient equations, each linear in the u j,
and either linear or quadratic in the other variables (w j or ξ j
as it may be). Eq. (L3c) are linear in the w j and ξ j and have
degree m in the u j. Accordingly, an upper bound for the isolated
solutions to this system of equations is the coefﬁcient of x2y2z2
in the formal product
(x + z)(2x + z)(y + z)(2y + z)(x + y + mz)2,
which is 4m2 + 24m + 22. This upper bound grows rapidly
with m, and in addition one must recall that the system of
equations will have solutions that are not relative equilibrium
conﬁgurations. Experience suggests that these “spurious”
solutions greatly outnumber the physically relevant ones. For
example, numerical solution (via a Groebner basis) of the
system (L3) for m = 3 and various circulations have suggested
that no more than 22 relative equilibrium conﬁgurations exist,
whereas the Bezout bound is 130.
A somewhat tighter bound, m2 + 6m + 2, can be obtained
for “symmetrical” conﬁgurations, in which the w j are real, by
using only Eqs. (L3a) and (L3c) with the substitution of w j
for ξ j throughout. For m = 3 this is not too much bigger than
the number of conﬁgurations (14) that actually exist for some
circulation choices. It seems clear however that the spurious
solutions to Eq. (L3) greatly outnumber the solutions that
correspond to relative equilibria, especially for larger m. This
situation may be compared with that found for homogeneous
triple rings [3], where the number of conﬁgurations stabilizes
for large m. Thus a Groebner basis approach to solving these
equations, although having the beneﬁt of completeness, would
be quite inefﬁcient.
